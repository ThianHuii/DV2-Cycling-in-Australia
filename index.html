What the comparisons tell us

A national minority.
A local opportunity.

Cycling’s national share gives only part of the picture. The territory comparisons, Melbourne-area LGAs and Sydney counter patterns show why a single national number cannot describe every community.

For readers in Malaysia, the useful question is local: where could everyday destinations be connected by practical cycling routes? These Australian observations offer comparisons to consider, rather than evidence that one policy will work everywhere.

Data & acknowledgements

The page compares different measures: people, bicycle passages, GPS points and normalised monthly counts. Each figure identifies its period and population. The 2021 Census was affected by pandemic travel restrictions and home working. Historical counter and GPS extracts describe their stated periods, not current cycling levels.

  1. Australian Bureau of Statistics — Australia’s journey to work. Main-mode Census comparisons for figures 1–2.
  2. ABS Census DataPacks. Working Population Profiles (W22 and bicycle-only state comparisons), and General Community Profile G62 for figures 3–7. Workplace geography and usual-residence geography are kept distinct.
  3. Transport for NSW — Cycling Count. Historical counter extracts supplied for figures 9–11; the prepared CSV files define the values and periods used here.
  4. Transport for NSW — Cycling Data. RiderLog/Bicycle Network GPS context for the supplied 2010–2013 extract in figure 8. This is a historical participating-rider sample, rather than a population estimate.
  5. Australian state boundary GeoJSON — Rowan Hogan repository. Local simplified copy used for the two Australia maps; background outlines do not encode population.

Prepared data: The webpage retains the supplied chart values. The Sydney grid sums GPS point records within cells; distinct journey counts are not summed. The radial index compares each counter with its own 12-month average. Regional contributions use the five groups in the supplied summary.
